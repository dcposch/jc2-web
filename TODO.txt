Decisions taken (2026-09-12)
- Stack: Astro. Entries are plain Markdown, not MDX, because MDX parses the
  braces in LaTeX as JavaScript. Maths renders to KaTeX at build time, so the
  site ships no maths JavaScript; invalid LaTeX fails the build. Figures are
  generated by scripts/figures.py and inlined as SVG through a :::figure
  directive, so they follow the reader's light/dark theme.
- First release: home page plus the Background entry. The two mathematical
  essays follow once their claims are checked against AUDIT.md and jc2-lean.
- Figures: static SVG first. Promote individual figures to interactive only
  where the interaction itself teaches something.
- Dates: entries display their historical date, and the feed uses it too.
- Proposed rule, not yet enforced: no entry publishes unless every theorem
  statement links to a Lean declaration, an AUDIT.md entry with its evidence
  tier, or a cited paper, and the prose says which of the three it is.
- Contributing will run through open-sourcing the root jc2/ repository, not
  jc2-lean. The mathematics runs well ahead of its formalization and JC2 may be
  settled long before it is verified in Lean, so the contribution surface has to
  be the research repo rather than the proof-assistant one. The homepage section
  stays a TODO until that repo is ready.

Content outline and editorial direction
=======================================

Draft content outline for jc2.fun

The site should give readers mathematical ideas they can understand, use, and develop. Each article should have lasting value whether or not the campaign resolves JC2. The intended audience spans readers comfortable with college mathematics and researchers who want complete statements, proofs, and reusable methods.

One shared single-column layout supports a short home page and a growing collection of dated entries. Figures may extend beyond the text column on desktop and fit the viewport on mobile. The home page is the chronological index, ordered oldest first so the Background entry begins the sequence. Use “entries” or “notes” in site copy. Keep navigation minimal: the site name links home; About and RSS can live in the footer.

- **Home — Mathematical insights on the road to JC2.** State the problem in a sentence and an equation. Introduce local versus global invertibility through a small interactive example. Explain why we are publishing intermediate mathematics. Give a short, dated account of what is known and what remains open. Below this introduction, list entries chronologically: date, linked title, and at most one short descriptive sentence. New entries append to the list. This single list provides the reading sequence; no separate recommended path or featured section is needed.

- **Background: reversible maps and JC2.** The first dated entry. Adapt the existing background draft: linear algebra; polynomial shears; the complex plane; the inverse function theorem; escape to infinity; counting inverse sheets; curves, Newton polygons, and Puiseux expansions. Describe the prior literature with explicit attribution. Keep this article a stable introduction to the problem, with links onward to the campaign's contributions.

- **Entries — One mathematical idea per essay.** Each entry has a date, a title, a permanent URL, and previous/next links. Background, discoveries, connections, failed approaches, and open questions all belong to the same chronological series. The subjects below describe planned material; dates determine their display order, and an entry appears on the public index when it is ready to read.

  - **When a polynomial differential equation forces a straight line.** Explain the rigidity identity \(AC'-\nu A'C=c\ne0\), starting with its simplest cases. Show the elementary leading-term cancellation proof and why it can control polynomial maps. Distinguish the known characteristic-zero theorem from the campaign's independent proof, its formalization, and the positive-characteristic extension.

  - **How a gap in a Newton polygon forces coefficients to vanish.** Work through one small strip example, deriving its equations from the Jacobian identity. Explain the finite block, the cancellation cascade, and exactly which family it excludes. Continue to the logarithmic-residue condition as the deeper extension. Visual: exponent points linked to the coefficient equations they produce.

  - **Why adjoining a cube root can simplify a problem.** Introduce the leading pair \(H^2,H^3\) at partial degrees \((6,9)\). Explain how cubic symmetry forces alignment and organizes the remaining equations. Develop the connection between field extensions, differentiation, and obstruction theory. State the precise scope of the noncube result and the separate obligations on the cube branch. Visual: three conjugate roots and the expressions preserved by their permutation.

  - **What the curve of escaping values is allowed to look like.** Build on the background's asymptotic curve. Explain the fibre-counting and cusp arguments through the simplest useful models, leading to the campaign's exclusion of a non-properness set homeomorphic to \(\mathbb C\). Separate the source curve from its image, and explain why a singularity of the image need not be a critical point of the original map. Visual: normalization, punctures, and the local inverse sheets.

  - **Which symmetries survive as a branch develops?** Explain Puiseux root clusters and how successive terms change their stabilizers. Show why the actual centre of a cluster matters, using a worked example before the general screen. Present the arithmetic census as an application of the mechanism, with exact scope and reproducible input data. Visual: a branching tree whose root-of-unity symmetries change at each step.

  - **What an infinite family teaches us about the limits of counting.** Exhibit a family satisfying the numerical constraints and explain what those constraints leave undetermined. Distinguish an admissible pattern from an actual polynomial map. Describe the reduction of the K=16 family to one polynomial differential-equation problem, including proved special cases and the unresolved uniform statement. Give this negative result and its new question the same expository attention as an exclusion theorem.

- **Connections and smaller discoveries.** Publish short notes when a useful idea does not need a full flagship essay: polynomial ODEs and residues; algebraic extensions and symmetry; monodromy and permutation groups; ramification and Euler characteristic; combinatorial configurations and algebraic realizability. Each should explain a concrete bridge, supply an example, and identify an application or follow-up question. Include independently rediscovered classical results when the new explanation is useful, with clear credit to the earlier work.

- **Approaches that taught us something.** Explain selected failed conjectures, tempting shortcuts, and corrected arguments. Preserve the initial motivation, give the decisive counterexample or obstruction, and derive the narrower statement that survives. Candidate subjects include equation counting, the difference between formal and polynomial solutions, and what a modular calculation can certify. Link corrections from the affected articles and retain dated versions.

- **Open problems — Questions a reader can take away.** Include these within essays or as standalone dated entries. Give each question a precise statement, motivation, a small example, known partial results, and links to the relevant essay and data. Include accessible exercises, computational experiments, and research problems; distinguish their expected level. Initial research candidates concern uniform polynomial-ODE obstructions, realization of admissible branch data, and the compatibility conditions missing from numerical screens. State whether answering a question would advance JC2 directly or be interesting independently.

- **Proofs, experiments, and reusable material.** Put exact theorem statements and their hypotheses alongside the exposition. Link full proofs, specific Lean declarations where available, small runnable computations, and certificate data from the relevant entry. Supply stable links for citation. Clearly distinguish a proof, a conditional theorem, a conjecture, and computational evidence; specify the scope of every formalized result.

- **About and participation.** Explain the human and AI contributions, how conjectures are tested and results reviewed, and how we track prior work and corrections. Credit contributors by contribution. Offer a simple way to report an error, suggest a connection, or contribute a proof or example. Provide an RSS feed and dated updates so readers can follow the mathematics over time.

Editorial pattern for an essay:

- Open with a concrete question or example and explain why it is worth understanding.
- Develop the mechanism before introducing the full machinery; define notation when it first matters.
- Give the exact statement and hypotheses, followed by the proof idea and enough detail to assess it. Longer derivations can live in linked appendices.
- Include discovery history where it explains a choice, a surprise, or a false start. Label reconstructed explanations as such; do not invent a discovery narrative.
- Credit the prior result at the point where it enters. Explain whether our contribution is a new statement, a new proof, a generalization, a computation, or an exposition.
- End with limitations and a concrete next question or experiment. Use the mathematics to create the sense of play: examples to vary, symmetries to notice, and predictions to test.

Presentation constraints:

- Comfortable text measure, generous spacing, readable mathematics, restrained color, and descriptive links.
- Figures embedded where the argument needs them, with desktop width beyond the text column when useful. All figures and controls remain usable on mobile.
- Interactive figures explain their parameters, give a useful initial state, support keyboard input, and include a static explanation. Honor reduced-motion preferences.
- Each essay's opening should reward a reader who stops after a few paragraphs; the full article should reward a specialist who follows every detail.
